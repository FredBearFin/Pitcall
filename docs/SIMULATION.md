# Simulation study: does Pitcall's number beat gut feel?

Status: Oct 25, 2026 (evening). Generated by `PITCALL_SIM=1 npx vitest run src/sim.report.test.ts` (code: `src/sim.ts`). Regenerate it
after any change to the forecast or count math.

**Read this first.** This is a model, not evidence from a real kitchen. It shows the app's math behaves sensibly and finds
bugs; it does not prove the app makes a real joint money. The model's demand shape (lognormal nights, a weekday pattern,
independent days) is close to what the engine itself assumes, so some of the engine's good showing is circular. Real nights
(a joint's own sales, 86 times, leftovers) are the only real test. Use these numbers to compare versions of the app and to
spot problems, not as a sales claim.

## What was simulated

- 150 restaurants per scenario, 70 nights each, five meats (the seed meats: brisket, butt, ribs, sausage, turkey), each
  starting on a random weekday.
- True demand per meat per night = usual-night guess x a weekday factor (Sun 1.05, Mon 0.6, Tue 0.7, Wed 0.8, Thu 0.95,
  Fri 1.4, Sat 1.5, rescaled to average 1) x lognormal noise (CV 30% unless noted). Freak nights, forgotten 86 taps, and a
  mismatched arrival curve are switched on in the rows below.
- Two worlds see identical demand each night. In the **Pitcall world** the cook follows Pitcall's count and the real
  pipeline learns from the closed rows (`computeMeatCount`, `fitToSlots`, `buildHistory`, `recoverDemand`). In the **owner
  world** the owner cooks by gut: the true average for that weekday x a bias x a nightly wobble (the "balanced" owner has no
  bias and a +-20% wobble). The smoker holds 32 slots unless noted.
- Profit is the app's own `realizedProfit` against the true demand (selling, leftovers worth their outlet value, cost of
  every cooked pound). Differences below are Pitcall minus owner, dollars per night, all five meats together.
- **Lost-sale share.** When a meat runs out, only some of those customers truly walk away; the rest order another meat. The
  true share is a scenario setting (default 1, every sell-out fully lost). The app is told its own share through
  `settings.lostSaleShare` (default 0.7). Rows marked "OLD MATH" tell the app 1.0.

## What this says

1. **Over-producing meat was a real defect, and it was an unexamined assumption, not a bug in the arithmetic.** The original
   math treated every sell-out as a fully lost sale. If only half of those customers really walk away (the rest buy ribs or
   sausage), that math cooks so much extra that Pitcall loses $33 a night to the owner's gut and only 2% of restaurants come
   out ahead (rows "OLD MATH ... only 50%"). Nobody has measured the real share, so it is now a setting (`lostSaleShare`, Settings screen) with a default of 0.7.
   - At the default, if every sell-out really is a lost sale, nothing is given up (+$69 a night versus +$68 before).
   - If half are really lost it is +$6 a night (was -$33). If only a quarter are lost it is still -$26: the share matters.
   - Pitcall at the default still cooks roughly twice the leftover of a balanced owner (37 lb a night across five meats versus
     19) in exchange for fewer 86s (about 35% of nights versus 51%). An owner who hates waste should lower the setting. In an
     earlier grid (same math, shares set directly), telling the app 0.55 when the truth was 1.0 still earned +$30 a night, and
     0.55 was never worse than +$30 whatever the true share. A defensible default could be lower than 0.7 once real data exists.
2. **Against an owner who knows an average night, Pitcall gains about $50-70 a night after a month (3-4% of profit).** The gain
   comes from running out less. Against a **sharp owner** (small errors, a sensible cushion) it is roughly break-even
   (+$14 a night at best, and behind or level for the first four weeks). The product is worth most to the owner whose gut is off by
   more than 10-15%, or who systematically over- or under-cooks. It will not turn a good pitmaster into a better one.
3. **The first week is the weak spot.** With no history the app leans on the typed "usual night" guess. If that guess is 30%
   too high Pitcall loses about $82 a night to the owner in week 1 (it over-cooks), and 67% too high loses about $161. The
   14-service-night free period covers this, but a joint that quits after a bad first week is the realistic way the app fails.
4. **A bad guess no longer lingers** (commit `caca197`). Before, a guess 30% too high still lost to the owner through night 28
   and only about broke even over nights 29-70. Now the level is corrected from the second week and that row is +$70 (99%
   ahead). Revert that one commit to restore the old behaviour.
5. **The weekly "Pitcall found you $X" figure is unreliable week to week.** It is a deliberately cautious lower bound: when a
   meat runs out the app cannot see what would have sold, so it counts the worst case against itself. In the simulation that
   bound is negative in over half of weeks (57% in the base case) even though the true gain over 70 nights is +$4,000. Because
   Pitcall now cooks less than before, the bound is lower than it was (the 70-night figure in the base row went from +$2,440
   to -$487). Two things were done: a night that closes with essentially nothing left now counts as a sell-out even if 86 was
   never tapped (commit `a3439a5`; before, a forgotten 86 could make the figure overstate by 44%), and the Week screen no longer
   prints a negative figure as "Pitcall found you -$X" in red. It says "Pitcall can't prove a gain this week". Using the
   app's own recovered-demand estimate instead of the worst case gives a much closer figure (about +$1,850 against +$4,000
   in the base row) but overclaims when taps are forgotten, so it was not adopted. The better fix is to show a trailing
   4-week figure; that is a product decision, listed in `docs/launch/LAUNCH-CHECKLIST.md`.
6. **Forgotten 86 taps cost little** in these runs (the app still wins), because most sell-outs also show up as zero leftover. A
   forgotten tap still makes the forecast read a sold-out night as exact demand. Left open: treat "zero leftover, no tap" as
   a censored night with an unknown time.
7. **Very noisy demand (CV 50%) is hard for everyone**: Pitcall's edge shrinks to about $13 and only 64% of restaurants
   are ahead.

## Things this study did not test

- Real owners adapting (cooking more before a holiday, reading the weather). The owner here is a fixed rule.
- Trends, seasons, holidays, and events marked by the owner. The forecast supports event tags; the Tonight screen does not
  expose them, so they are not simulated.
- Catering, mid-service cooks, carry-over meat from yesterday (the Z-hardened build's "carry-in" idea is not in this app).
- Whether people will actually follow Pitcall's number, or type their own first and then stay with it.
- Real leftover handling. Overnight-cooked meat held for a second day, chopped, or binned has food-safety and quality costs the
  model only prices through the leftover value the owner types.
- The cook-time and put-on-time half of the app. This study is about how many to cook, not when.

## Invariant soak test

`src/soak.test.ts` is part of the normal suite (about 8 seconds). It runs 70-night simulated restaurants under hostile
conditions (a guess 2.5x too low or 60% too high, 25% freak nights, never tapping 86, a 6-slot smoker, CV 100%) and checks every
night: finite numbers, sigma at least 1 lb, whole non-negative pieces, headline count inside the good range, total slots never
over the smoker. Two aggregate guards: Pitcall stays ahead of a balanced owner over nights 29-70 (80% of restaurants), and the
weekly "found you" figure never exceeds the simulated truth when 86 taps are forgotten.
